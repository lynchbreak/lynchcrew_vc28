Days 1–3 · 3 nights

Hanoi

The place

A working capital of about 8.5 million that happens to contain a medieval merchant quarter. Not a museum city and not a resort: it’s the political centre of Vietnam and behaves like one — formal, northern in manner, more reserved than Saigon and warmer once engaged. Life happens on the pavement; the plastic-stool economy of pho, bun cha, bia hoi and coffee is genuinely how people eat, not a performance.

The history, properly. Emperor Ly Thai To moved the capital here in 1010 and named it Thang Long, “ascending dragon.” Craftsmen clustered east of the citadel to supply the court — that settlement is the Old Quarter, and its streets still carry guild names: Hang Bac (silver), Hang Gai (silk), Hang Ma (votive paper). The Nguyen took the capital to Hue in 1802; Minh Mang renamed the city Ha Noi, “inside the river,” in 1831. The French made it capital of all Indochina in 1902 — which is why there’s a whole second city of boulevards and villas south of the Old Quarter. Ho Chi Minh declared independence at Ba Dinh Square on 2 September 1945; the city was bombed in the Linebacker II raids of December 1972; and almost everything commercially visible today postdates the 1986 doi moi reforms.

Good at, and not. Outstanding at street food, coffee, texture and the sensation of being somewhere genuinely foreign. Good at short, sharp historical sites. Not good at grand set-pieces — nothing here is Angkor — and not relaxing in the resort sense: noise, traffic and air quality are real, and February brings the crachin, a cool grey drizzle around 15–20°C with about an hour and a half of sun a day.

Unstructured time: the best wandering city on the trip. The Old Quarter rewards aimlessness because it has no plan — tube houses three metres wide and forty deep, trades changing every hundred metres, temples wedged between hardware shops. Egg coffee was invented here in 1946 at the Metropole during a milk shortage, and Cafe Giang still trades on Nguyen Huu Huan. Three nights is right: one lost to arrival, one for a guide, one entirely free.

Our appraisal

What it is. Fifty minutes in a small, packed theatre. Experience — Vietnam Museum of Ethnology, $3£2pp

The best museum in Vietnam, routinely missed: all 54 ethnic groups indoors, ten full-scale village houses in the garden.

Our appraisal

What it is. Two parts. Indoors, a well-curated, properly labelled (Vietnamese, French and English — rare here) survey of all 54 recognised ethnic groups. Outdoors — the real reason to come — a large garden of ten full-scale traditional houses dismantled in their home villages and rebuilt here by craftsmen from those communities: a 19-metre Bahnar communal house with a soaring thatched roof, an Ede longhouse you walk the length of, a Giarai tomb with carved mourning figures. Two and a half hours. Closed Mondays. About 25 minutes by taxi from the Old Quarter.

Why it matters. Vietnam’s national story is overwhelmingly Kinh and overwhelmingly about war. This is the one major institution arguing the country is a plural place with its own highland histories and cosmologies — and it’s far and away the best-run museum in Vietnam.

Verdict — the one thing to add to Hanoi. Cheap, uncrowded, shaded, and the outdoor houses make it a walk in a garden rather than a gallery slog — which suits a downtime trip far better than another temple. The cost is the 8 km location: a half-day with transfers. Do it as the slow morning after a late night, skip the indoor galleries if attention flags, and be back for lunch. If you do one museum in Hanoi, this one.

Experience — Thang Long Imperial Citadel, $6£3pp

A thousand years of Vietnamese governance on one site — and the Cold War bunker where the Tet Offensive was planned.

Our appraisal

What it is. The site of the citadel of Thang Long, political centre of Vietnam from 1010 until the capital moved to Hue in 1802. The French demolished most of it in the 1890s, so above ground you see the south gate, the Flag Tower, the dragon staircase of a palace that’s gone, and an open archaeological pit across the road exposing foundations from the 7th to the 19th centuries. And the thing almost nobody mentions: the D67 bunker, the blast-doored underground headquarters where Giap and the Politburo planned the 1968 Tet Offensive and the 1975 campaign, preserved with its maps, phones and furniture. Central, two hours, closed Mondays.

Why it matters. It’s the only place you can stand on Ly dynasty foundations under French colonial buildings under a Cold War command post, all within two hundred metres. UNESCO listed it in 2010 for exactly that layering.

Verdict — worth two hours, for the bunker. Without a guide the above-ground part reads as lawns with signage and you’ll be bored in forty minutes. With one: 30 minutes above ground, 30 in the pit, 45 in D67, then leave. It’s also one of the few genuinely calm spaces in central Hanoi. If you’re choosing one historical site, choose the Ethnology Museum; if two, add this. Skip the Mausoleum queue entirely — this is the better use of that hour.

Days 4–5 · 2 nights afloat

Lan Ha Bay

The place

The quieter southern lobe of the Ha Long karst seascape, licensed to far fewer boats than the core bay — which takes nine million visitors a year, five hundred vessels on a peak day, and 30–45-minute cave queues. Lan Ha is the right choice over Ha Long proper. Same drowned limestone towers, a fraction of the traffic.

February, honestly. The operators’ blogs say “ideal”; independent sources say fog. Both are partly right. Cancellation risk is near zero — no storms, no monsoon. Visibility is genuinely compromised: expect mist, especially mornings, and near-constant overcast. You will not be fogged out, but you probably won’t get blue sky either. You get moody, silvered, mist-wreathed karst, which photographs better than harsh midday haze. The sea is 18–20°C, so kayaking, swimming and beach stops — the three things the second night is normally sold on — are off. The second night becomes about scenery, food and the sundeck in a jacket.

Unstructured time: fully operator-run, which is the point. You make no decisions for 48 hours. Skip the Sung Sot cave queue if it’s offered. The pearl farm and squid fishing are filler. Two nights is right — one is almost entirely transit — but don’t go to three on a boat in February.

Day 7 · 1 night

Hue

The place

A quiet, slightly melancholy provincial city of about 650,000 on the Perfume River — green, low-rise, faded, unhurried, and home to the most refined and most chilli-forward cooking in Vietnam. It feels unlike anywhere else in the country.

The history, properly. Seat of the Nguyen lords from 1687; imperial capital of a unified Vietnam from 1802, when Gia Long took the throne and built the Citadel on a Vauban-influenced square, 2.5 km a side, with the Forbidden Purple City at its heart for the emperor alone. Thirteen emperors reigned here until Bao Dai abdicated on 30 August 1945. Then the Battle of Hue, 31 January to 2 March 1968 — the longest urban battle of the war, fought room by room inside the walls. Of around 160 major structures in the Imperial City, about ten survived. The Forbidden Purple City is, to this day, largely foundations and grass. Separately, during the month of occupation, several thousand officials, teachers and civilians were systematically executed — the Hue Massacre, with mass graves recovered afterwards.

Reconstruction versus original. Be clear-eyed: much of what you walk through is rebuilt. The walls, the Noon Gate, Thai Hoa Palace and the Nine Dynastic Urns are substantially original; most inner palaces are post-1993 reconstruction or empty ground. The royal tombs, by contrast, were outside the battle and are largely authentic — which makes them the better use of limited time.

Unstructured time: a scheduled stop, not a wandering one. The riverfront and Dong Ba market are pleasant but thin. February is Hue’s worst month — cool, persistently grey, the wettest central city even in the dry season. One night is defensible as transit-with-purpose; it’s not a rest night, and a good guide is more than half the value here.

Experience — Royal-cuisine dinner at Ancient Hue, $46£24pp

The Nguyen court’s cuisine — seven to ten small, carved, symmetrical courses — in a restored garden-house compound in Kim Long. No costumes.

Our appraisal

What it is. Ẩm thực cung đình Huế — the court cuisine codified under emperors who reputedly required fifty dishes a meal, each tiny. In practice, a two-hour set menu: a carved phoenix of vegetables, shrimp paste on sugarcane, stuffed pigeon, lotus-seed rice, a clear soup, a sweet. Presentation is the point.

Why it matters. Hue is the only Vietnamese city with an aristocratic culinary tradition, because it’s the only one that had an aristocracy. The technique survived the dynasty’s fall in families who cooked for the court.

Verdict — worth doing once, and choose the register carefully. The failure mode is the costumed banquet — polyester mandarin robes, a cardboard hat, a hired ensemble, indifferent food at tourist pace. For a premium group that’s excruciating. Ancient Hue is the serious kitchen in a beautiful compound and nobody puts a hat on you; Y Thao Garden is the charming family-run version at a third of the price. The honest counter-argument: Hue’s best food is bún bò Huế at a street stall at 7am, and it beats any court menu. Do one royal dinner for the occasion and one street-food morning for the food.

Days 8–12 · 5 nights

Hoi An

The place

A small, exceptionally preserved 17th–19th-century merchant town of yellow-washed shophouses, attached to 30 km of beach and surrounded by paddy, river islands and craft villages. It is also, honestly, Vietnam’s most intensively touristed place: roughly five million visitors a year against 120,000 residents, and between 4pm and 10pm the core streets are shoulder to shoulder.

The history, properly. A Cham port from around the 2nd century, trading silk, pearls and aloeswood with Arab and Chinese merchants. The golden age ran roughly 1570–1700 under the Nguyen lords, who opened the port — Faifo, to Europeans — to fund their war against the north. Japanese, Chinese, Dutch and Portuguese traders worked here; the Japanese held their own quarter, and the Japanese Covered Bridge literally links it to the Chinese one. The Japanese left abruptly after the Tokugawa closed-country edicts of 1635–39; the Chinese stayed and built five assembly halls by home province, still the town’s best buildings. Then the decisive thing happened slowly: the Thu Bon river silted, ships could no longer reach the wharves, and trade moved to deep-water Da Nang. That stagnation is the whole reason the town exists — with no reason to rebuild, 1,068 houses simply stayed. UNESCO listed it in December 1999.

Good at, and not. Superb at food (cao lau, mi quang, white-rose dumplings — Quang Nam has its own cuisine), tailoring, cycling, and the plain visual pleasure of the lanterns coming on at dusk. The craft villages — Thanh Ha pottery, Tra Que herbs — are genuinely worthwhile. What it isn’t: quiet, or undiscovered. Residents are steadily moving out, which is hollowing the thing that made it alive. February is dry and 24–26°C, but the sea is 21–23°C and the sky is often grey: good for the town, not reliable for the beach.

Unstructured time: this is where five nights earns its keep — if you treat the Ancient Town as a visit and the surroundings as the stay. The old streets are wonderful at 6–8am when the market is working and the lanes are empty, and again after 9:30pm. Midday they’re a shopping crowd. The real unstructured reward is the bicycle: flat lanes through paddy to the beach, riverside tracks, Cam Kim island, An Bang cafés. Five nights with a pool, a few beach afternoons, a tailoring cycle that naturally spreads over three days, one dawn walk and one evening one, is a strong downtime allocation.

Days 13–14 · 2 nights

Saigon

The place

Vietnam’s commercial engine — around nine million people, a quarter of national GDP. Faster, hotter, richer, louder and more outward-facing than Hanoi; it’s where Vietnamese capitalism is most visible. Everyone still says Saigon. Note the numbered districts were formally abolished in July 2025, but hotels, taxis and locals all still use them.

The history, properly. A Khmer river port called Prey Nokor, “forest city,” by the 12th century. Vietnamese settlement followed a 1620 dynastic marriage; in 1698 the Nguyen lords formally detached the region from Cambodia — which is why Cambodian nationalists still call the south Kampuchea Krom. The French took the city by force in 1859 and made it capital of Cochinchina, a directly ruled colony unlike the northern protectorates, which is why the colonial imprint here is heavier and more confident than anywhere else: the Haussmann grid, the Post Office, Notre-Dame, the Opera House. Capital of the Republic of Vietnam 1955–75 and the centre of the American presence; attacked in the 1968 Tet Offensive; fell on 30 April 1975 — the Independence Palace gates are the exact spot. Renamed in 1976. Cholon, “big market,” was founded by Chinese refugees in the 1770s and was a separate city until 1931; its community was badly hit by the post-1975 nationalisations and the boat-people exodus.

Good at, and not. Excellent at food at every price, coffee, bars and rooftops, and two of the most affecting war-history sites in Southeast Asia — the War Remnants Museum and the Independence Palace, preserved as it was on 30 April 1975. Not good at tranquillity, walking in the heat, or beauty at scale. February is peak dry season: 32–34°C, humid, bright, poor air.

Unstructured time: a scheduled city, with one exception. District 1 is a short wander — the colonial core, Nguyen Hue, the apartment-block cafés at 42 Nguyen Hue. District 3 is the better aimless walk: tree-lined, low-rise, dense with good coffee and few tourists. Cholon is the most rewarding wander for anyone curious, but you need a guide to make it legible. Two nights is correct: one full day. Do the heavy history with a guide in the morning, let the evening run free.

Days 15–16 · 2 nights

Mekong Delta

The place

Forty thousand square kilometres of alluvial floodplain a metre or two above sea level, home to 17 million people, laced with thousands of kilometres of canals — more a water-landscape with towns in it than a region with rivers. Can Tho is its working capital: a humid, flat, pleasant river port that makes no attempt to charm you.

The history, properly. Geologically young — built by Mekong sediment over about six thousand years, and until recently still building seaward. Khmer land, with the Funan-era port of Oc Eo trading with Rome and India in the first centuries CE, until Vietnamese expansion from the late 17th century. The French from the 1860s turned it into a rice-export machine, dredging a canal web across the delta from 1875; rice area went from 522,000 hectares in 1880 to 2.4 million by 1930. Every canal you see from a boat is colonial infrastructure. It was then Viet Cong heartland, heavily bombed and defoliated, and it remains Vietnam’s rice bowl.

The contemporary picture, honestly. The delta is in slow crisis. Upstream dams trap more than half the historical sediment, so it’s stopped rebuilding itself. Groundwater extraction is sinking the land faster than the sea is rising. Saltwater now reaches 40–70 km inland each dry season — February is squarely in it. And the floating markets are a direct casualty, though of prosperity more than ecology: Cai Rang had 400-plus trading boats in the 1990s, about 200 registered now, 30–40 actually trading on a given morning. Roads reached the villages and trucks beat boats on cost.

Is it worth two nights? Yes — but reframe what you’re there for. If the answer is “the floating market,” one night is plenty and you may be mildly disappointed. If it’s “time in a landscape and a way of life that is both extraordinary and measurably disappearing,” two nights on a private islet with nothing scheduled is exactly right. Can Tho city is not a wandering destination; the honest pleasure of the delta is passive — a verandah over brown water, sampans passing, the heat. By 11am it’s too hot to do anything but nothing, which, given the brief, is the point.

Days 17–18 · 2 nights

Phnom Penh

The place

A low-rise riverside capital of about 2.3 million at the confluence of the Mekong, Tonle Sap and Bassac. Scruffier, poorer and more chaotic than Saigon, but more intimate, and at the riverfront and in the French quarter genuinely charming. A construction boom bolted onto a city still recovering from an annihilation within living memory.

The history, properly. Legend dates it to 1372 and a widow named Penh who raised a shrine on a hill — Wat Phnom, “Penh’s hill.” King Ponhea Yat moved the capital here in 1432 after Ayutthaya sacked Angkor; it held the capital only 73 years, then was abandoned for three and a half centuries. Permanent capital again from 1865 under the French protectorate, which laid the grid, built the quays and the villas, and gave it the tag “Pearl of Asia.” The Sihanouk years, 1955–70, produced the extraordinary New Khmer Architecture of Vann Molyvann — the Independence Monument, the Olympic Stadium — one of the most underrated things to look at in the city. Then on 17 April 1975 the Khmer Rouge entered and within days forcibly emptied it entirely: the complete evacuation of a capital, a defining act of a regime that killed roughly 1.7–2 million people — a quarter of the country — in under four years. Tuol Sleng and Choeung Ek are both here. Vietnamese forces took the city on 7 January 1979; it repopulated chaotically through the 1980s, a legacy still visible in land disputes today.

Good at, and not. Riverfront life, markets, very good and underrated food, the Palace, and the National Museum — the best Khmer sculpture collection anywhere; see it before Angkor and it will transform the temples. Not good at comfort, air, traffic, or pavements you can walk on. The genocide sites are essential and genuinely harrowing — budget recovery time.

Unstructured time: works here in short doses. The riverfront at dusk when the whole city comes out, the lanes around Wat Botum and BKK1, the French quarter north of the Post Office. Not a wandering city at Hanoi’s level — heat and tuk-tuk touting interrupt — but an evening with no plan on Sisowath Quay is one of the pleasanter hours in Southeast Asia. Two nights is exactly right: one for the heavy history, one for the city, with the evenings open.

Building A holds the iron bed frames of the last fourteen victims, photographed where they were found in January 1979, the photographs hung in the rooms. Building B holds the mugshot archive — thousands of faces, every prisoner photographed on arrival. Building C still has its anti-suicide barbed wire. The audio guide is narrated partly by survivors.

Why it matters. Of an estimated 18,000 people processed here, fewer than fifteen survived. It’s the most completely documented site of the genocide precisely because the regime was bureaucratically obsessive — it photographed and filed everyone it killed.

Verdict — unquestionably worth it, and one of the most important places you’ll visit anywhere. Pay for the audio guide; it isn’t optional. Without it this is empty rooms; with it, unbearable and necessary. The mugshot archive is the part that undoes people — the ordinariness of the faces. This is not an activity, it’s an ordeal, and it will set the tone for the day. Go in the morning, schedule nothing demanding after, and don’t book a cheerful dinner that night. Four people will process it at different speeds: agree in advance that you’ll separate inside and regroup at the gate.

Experience — Choeung Ek killing fields, with audio guide and the half-day car, $20£11pp

Fifteen kilometres out. Entry is $9 with the audio guide included; the rest is your share of a car for the morning covering both sites.

Our appraisal

What it is. A former orchard where S-21 prisoners were trucked at night and killed — by farm tools, not bullets, which were too expensive. A raised walkway loops past excavated mass graves. Bone and cloth fragments still surface in the paths after rain. At the centre, a 1988 memorial stupa holds more than 5,000 skulls arranged by age and injury. The audio guide, including survivor testimony and an optional track of the revolutionary music played through loudspeakers to mask the sound, is widely considered among the best at any memorial site in the world.

Why it matters. Roughly 20,000 people were killed here; it’s one of some 300 identified killing sites across the country.

Verdict — worth it, and do it with S-21, in that order, the same morning. That’s the order the victims travelled and the order the two audio guides are designed around. Some argue for splitting them across two days; we don’t — it makes you do the emotional preparation twice and leaves the second half hanging over a day that should be free. 8am at S-21, drive out at 10:30, here 11:00–12:30, late lunch, then the entire rest of the day doing nothing. Wrong for anyone recently bereaved, and for anyone who’ll be troubled that the stupa has a gift shop.

Experience — Royal Palace & Silver Pagoda, $15£8pp incl. audio guide

The working residence of the King, and a pagoda floored with five thousand silver tiles. Ninety minutes. Open 8–11 and 2–5; shoulders and knees covered.

Our appraisal

What it is. The residence of King Norodom Sihamoni, built from 1866. The Throne Hall from outside and a roped-off interior view, the Napoleon III Pavilion — a cast-iron folly gifted by France — the gardens, then the Silver Pagoda: five thousand solid silver tiles of about a kilo each, mostly carpeted over, an Emerald Buddha in Baccarat crystal, and a 90-kilo gold Maitreya set with 2,086 diamonds. The private apartments are closed. It shuts over the middle of the day, which catches people out. Dress code is strictly enforced — sarongs are lent at the gate, but the queue is tedious; just dress correctly.

Why it matters. The clearest surviving expression of Khmer monarchy as a continuous institution — one the Khmer Rouge didn’t destroy, largely because Sihanouk was useful to them.

Verdict — worth ninety minutes, mainly as the counterweight. Phnom Penh shouldn’t be only an atrocity itinerary, and this is the city’s demonstration of a Cambodia older and larger than 1975. On its own merits it’s pleasant rather than remarkable — a lot of gilt, much of it 20th-century, much of it behind a rope. The Silver Pagoda is the memorable part. The trade-off is heat: open, reflective, shadeless. Go at 8, be out by 10, and walk two minutes to the National Museum. Never on the same day as the genocide sites.

Experience — National Museum of Cambodia, $15£8pp

The world’s largest collection of Khmer sculpture, in a cool terracotta pavilion around a lotus courtyard. One to one and a half hours.

Our appraisal

What it is. Pre-Angkorian Funan and Chenla work from the 6th–8th centuries, the great Angkorian bronzes and sandstone, and the post-Angkorian Buddhist material, in a 1917–20 pavilion built in the Khmer revival style. The signature pieces are the 6th-century Vishnu from Phnom Da, the reclining bronze Vishnu from the West Mebon, and the “Leper King” removed from Angkor Thom for protection. Looted pieces returned from the Met and the British Museum in the last decade are shown with that provenance stated. A live guide at the door is about $12 for the group — the best value on this page.

Why it matters. This is where the sculpture that was taken out of the Angkor temples lives. The temples you’ll walk through are stripped shells; the figures that filled them are here. See it before Siem Reap and the temples become legible.

Verdict — worth it, and better value than the Palace next door. The room-by-room progression from Funan to late Angkor makes the stylistic evolution clear in a way no amount of temple-walking does, and the building is cool, shaded and breezy — a genuine relief in a hot Phnom Penh morning. Labelling is thin, so hire the guide. Skip it only if sculpture galleries aren’t your thing, in which case the Palace alone and the hotel pool is a perfectly respectable morning.

Days 19–22 · 4 nights

Siem Reap & Angkor

The place

Two distinct places. Angkor is a 400 km² archaeological park containing what was, around 1200, probably the largest pre-industrial urban complex on earth — perhaps 750,000 people across a low-density hydraulic city. Siem Reap is a small, flat, pleasant service town of 250,000 that exists almost entirely to serve it, and outside the hotel strips and Pub Street is a genuinely nice provincial Cambodian town along the river.

The history, properly. The empire’s conventional founding is 802, when Jayavarman II declared himself universal monarch on Phnom Kulen. Suryavarman II built Angkor Wat around 1113–1150, dedicated to Vishnu and oriented west, almost certainly as his funerary temple. After the Cham sacked the city in 1177, Jayavarman VII — a Mahayana Buddhist, the great builder — raised Angkor Thom, the Bayon with its face-towers, Ta Prohm and Preah Khan, plus a network of 102 hospitals along the royal roads. The hydraulics are the real story: an engineered water landscape of enormous reservoirs and canals capturing the monsoon for dry-season rice. Sediment cores show severe multi-decade droughts in the 14th and 15th centuries, punctuated by violent flood years; the network silted, breached, and couldn’t be repaired at scale. Add the shift to Theravada Buddhism, which undercut the basis for vast royal temple-building, and the capital’s logic dissolved. Ayutthaya sacked it in 1431. It was never truly “lost” — monks maintained Angkor Wat continuously — but Henri Mouhot’s 1860 account drove the Western rediscovery and the great colonial restorations.

Good at, and not. The temples are, straightforwardly, world-class and among the few sites that exceed expectation. Visitor numbers are down, not up — international arrivals to the park fell again in 2025 — so it’s less crowded than its reputation, though sunrise at Angkor Wat is still a scrum. February is the ideal month: dry, 22–33°C, clear. What Siem Reap isn’t: historically interesting in itself. Pub Street is unapologetically a backpacker bar strip. And temple fatigue is real and sets in faster than people expect.

Unstructured time: four nights is right, provided it’s not four days of temples. Two heavy days plus a light outlying morning, and the rest genuinely open. The town is pleasant for low-key drifting — the Wat Bo and riverside lanes, Kandal Village for shops and cafés, the Old Market, good spas, excellent modern Khmer restaurants. Hotel pools here are a legitimate activity; the heat enforces an afternoon pause. It’s the most restful base on the trip.

Experience — Temple day one with a historian guide and private car, $250£133 for the four of you

Sunrise at Srah Srang instead of the Angkor Wat scrum, Ta Prohm at opening, the Bayon, then Angkor Wat itself in the late afternoon when its west face is actually lit.

Our appraisal

What it is, and what it costs. The original plan had a “private sunrise historian tour” at $110 a head — OTA pricing. Booked direct or through your hotel, a licensed English-speaking guide is about US$40–50 a day and a private car US$40–60, so the whole day for four is US$80–110. A specialist art-historian guide — there are perhaps a dozen in Siem Reap of genuine scholarly calibre — is a real premium at US$80–120 for the day and is what we’ve priced. Book that one.

Why it matters. Angkor Wat is the largest religious monument on earth, and its 600 metres of bas-relief — the Churning of the Sea of Milk, the Battle of Kurukshetra, the Judgement of Yama — are the finest narrative stone carving in Asia and the single strongest argument for a guide.

Verdict — the guide is essential; the Angkor Wat sunrise is overrated, and we’ve restructured the day around that. Roughly 8,000 people attend it daily, shoulder to shoulder in the dark holding phones above heads — and the temple faces west, so sunrise backlights it, which is why everyone shoots silhouettes. Do this instead: sunrise from Srah Srang, the royal bathing pool ten minutes away, with typically fewer than twenty people; walk into Ta Prohm at opening before any bus; the Bayon; back by 11 for the pool; then Angkor Wat from 3:30 to sunset, lit correctly and emptying out. Same alarm, a thousand fewer people, a better day. One early start on this leg, not three.

Experience — Banteay Srei at opening, then Preah Khan and Neak Pean, guide and car, $155£82 for the four of you

The finest carving in the Khmer world at 7:30 before the coaches, then the better half of the grand circuit. Back by 11 with the day free. Covered by the pass.

Our appraisal

What it is. Banteay Srei: a small 967 Shaivite temple 37 km out, built not by a king but by a Brahmin counsellor, in rose-pink sandstone harder and finer-grained than the grey of the great temples — which is why its carving survives in a condition nothing else at Angkor approaches. Lintels carved almost to the point of sculpture: the Ramayana, the burning of the Khandava forest, Shiva on Mount Kailasa. Doorways under five feet. Forty-five minutes on site, and it’s where André Malraux was arrested in 1923 for stealing four devatas. Then Preah Khan, Jayavarman VII’s 1191 monastic complex, 56 hectares half-consumed by jungle at a fifth of Ta Prohm’s visitor numbers, and Neak Pean, a tiny island hospital-temple reached on a boardwalk over flooded forest — a ten-minute walk that’s pure pleasure.

Why it matters. Banteay Srei is the single finest piece of stonework in the Khmer world and the thing art historians travel for. If we had to cut either this or the full grand circuit, we’d cut the circuit without hesitation.

Verdict — the best 45 minutes at Angkor, and the strongest small-effort, large-payoff item on this trip. Don’t skip it. The full grand circuit — Pre Rup, East Mebon, Ta Som on top — is where temple fatigue arrives; two of your four would be sitting in the van by the fourth temple. So we’ve trimmed it: Banteay Srei at 7:30 opening when you’ll have it nearly alone, Preah Khan for a slow hour, Neak Pean as the coda, and stop by 11. You’ll have had the better half of the circuit and the rest of the day free.

Experience — Phare, the Cambodian Circus, Section B, $43£23pp

Acrobatics, contortion and narrative in a 300-seat big-top, from a free arts school for disadvantaged kids that started in a refugee camp. 8pm nightly, 70 minutes.

Our appraisal

What it is. Not a circus with animals. Eight to ten young performers doing acrobatics, aerial work, tumbling and clowning in a small, hot tent, with live percussion and brass, telling a story — the repertoire rotates between shows about a girl’s recovery from trauma through art, rural poverty, land-grabbing and migration. Raw, physical, close up.

Why it matters. Phare Ponleu Selpak was founded in 1994 in Battambang by nine young men who’d been in the refugee camps on the Thai border and were taught drawing as therapy. It’s now a free school for 1,200 children, and the Siem Reap circus is its funding engine — performers are graduates on salaries. The narratives aren’t generic; they’re about the genocide.

Verdict — the best evening in Siem Reap by a wide margin, and one of the few things on this trip we’d book before anything else. It’s genuinely skilled, genuinely funny, and emotionally direct in a way no other performance in either country is. After a day of temples it’s exactly the right register, and the direct replacement for the cut Tonlé Sap trip and any “Apsara dinner.” Section B, not A — every seat is close and the $15 premium buys a souvenir. Avoid C; “partially obstructed” means it. Hot, firm benches, loud. Sells out in February; book ahead.

Experience — Beng Mealea half-day, private car, $115£61 for the four of you

A temple the size of Angkor Wat, essentially unrestored — collapsed galleries, trees through towers, explored on a boardwalk with some scrambling. 90 minutes each way, home by 2. Covered by the pass.

Our appraisal

What it is. A 12th-century temple 65 km out, never restored: sandstone blocks in heaps, galleries collapsed, trees growing out of towers. It’s the ruin-in-the-jungle fantasy that Ta Prohm, with its boardwalks and crowds, no longer delivers. The original plan’s idea was to pair it with Koh Ker — the empire’s capital for twenty years from 928, with a 36-metre seven-tiered pyramid that looks Mayan, landmined and inaccessible until the mid-2000s, UNESCO-listed in 2023, and a separate US$15 ticket. Spectacular.

Why it matters. It’s the clearest picture of what every temple at Angkor looked like before the French arrived.

Verdict — do Beng Mealea alone as a half-day; the Koh Ker version is almost certainly wrong for this group. Koh Ker is 120 km each way: a ten-hour day with five hours in a car on provincial roads, in February heat, for two couples who asked for downtime. That’s the whole day gone and most of the next recovering. Beng Mealea alone gives you 80% of the jungle-ruin experience for 40% of the day, home by 2. Keep Koh Ker only if one of you is a serious temple enthusiast and the others are happy to read in the car. Uneven scrambling — not ideal for dodgy knees. And if temple appetite hasn’t survived by day 22, the pool is the better outcome.

Experience — Tonlé Sap stilted-village boat trip, $70£37pp

A boat out to the floating villages on the lake. In the original plan; we recommend cutting it.

Our appraisal

What it is. A drive to the lakeshore and a boat out to one of the stilted or floating villages — Chong Khneas is the nearest and most visited; Kampong Phluk is better.

Why it matters. The Tonlé Sap is Southeast Asia’s largest freshwater lake and reverses its flow with the monsoon — genuinely remarkable hydrology, and the reason Angkor existed where it did.

Verdict — cut it. Chong Khneas is an outright tourist shakedown. Kampong Phluk is better, but in February the lake is at its dry-season low: the stilt villages look stranded rather than magical, the water is brown, and the boat ride is through mud. The hydrology is fascinating; the February experience isn’t. Phare is the better evening, and the pool is the better afternoon. Listed so you can decide, not because we think you should.
